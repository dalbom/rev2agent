# Phase 3: Research Plan Formulation

## Objective
Formalize the selected research direction into a concrete research plan: title, research question, hypothesis, proposed method, target datasets, evaluation metrics, and target venue.

## Mode
Direct conversation with the user. This phase requires careful deliberation and user buy-in.

## Inputs
- Selected direction from Phase 2
- Literature findings (read from `{project_dir}/literature/`)
- Baseline SOTA results

## Steps

### 3.1 Research Question Formulation

If the `/deep-research` skill is available, use it to conduct a targeted deep dive on the selected direction. Otherwise, use WebSearch to research: the specific method/approach chosen in Phase 2, its known limitations, recent advances, and unexplored variations. Use the findings to ensure the research question targets a genuine gap and avoids duplicating existing work.

Refine the broad direction into a precise research question. A good research question is:
- **Specific**: Not "Does synthetic data help?" but "Does training on GTA-V synthetic data with domain randomization improve Cityscapes semantic segmentation mIoU compared to training on Cityscapes alone?"
- **Testable**: Can be answered with experiments.
- **Novel**: Not already fully answered in existing literature.
- **Scoped**: Achievable within the available resources.

Present the research question and ask for confirmation.

### 3.2 Hypothesis

State the expected outcome clearly:
- "We hypothesize that augmenting the training set with synthetic data generated by [method] will improve [metric] on [dataset] by [expected margin], because [reasoning based on literature]."

### 3.3 Proposed Method

Outline the proposed approach at a high level:
- What is the core idea?
- How does it differ from existing work?
- What is the novelty claim?

Structure this as:
1. **Input**: What data goes in
2. **Process**: What transformations/training/methods are applied
3. **Output**: What the system produces
4. **Evaluation**: How success is measured

### 3.4 Target Venue and Paper Positioning

Based on the scope and novelty, recommend a target venue:
- Explain why this venue is appropriate.
- Note the submission deadline if known (search the web for current deadlines).
- Adjust the research plan's ambition to match the venue tier.

**Paper positioning (CRITICAL — must be decided here, not during manuscript writing):**
- Define in one sentence what the paper IS and what it IS NOT (e.g., "a practical CV tool, not a security paper").
- This determines which experiments matter, which claims are in scope, and how results are framed.
- If the venue is a CV conference, do not design experiments as if submitting to a security venue (and vice versa).
- Record the positioning statement in `.research_state.json` under `topic.positioning`.
- All subsequent phases (experiment design, manuscript writing, review) should reference this positioning.

### 3.5 Comparison Strategy

Define what the paper will compare against:
- **Baselines**: Vanilla model trained without the proposed approach.
- **Existing methods**: SOTA methods from the literature review.
- **Ablations**: Variants of the proposed method to understand which components matter.

**Comparison scope (CRITICAL):**
- Explicitly define which methods are comparable under the same conditions (e.g., "post-hoc methods only" or "methods that require no retraining").
- If a well-known method operates under different conditions (e.g., requires training-time integration), state upfront whether it will be included as a reference or excluded entirely.
- Do not defer this decision to manuscript writing — deciding comparison scope after experiments leads to unfair or awkward comparisons.

### 3.6 Dataset & Metric Selection

Finalize:
- **Training datasets**: Including any synthetic data sources.
- **Evaluation datasets**: Standard benchmarks for the target domain.
- **Metrics**: Standard metrics for the task (e.g., mIoU, mAP, FID).
- **Statistical rigor**: Multiple runs with different seeds, confidence intervals, significance tests.
